The confidence judge, measured

The guard-rail is built and shipped: a Haiku 4.5 judge reads every served Agent SDK turn's trace and decides whether the answer is released or withheld. It was trained the way sdk_v1 was — diagnose with the answer key, distil without it — gated on a calibrate split, and scored once on the sealed 349-turn gate split. This page reports what it actually does.

It does not hit the target you set. The expectation was high confidence = 99% accurate, stripping 99% of the incorrect answers. Measured on the test split, the high band is 91.5% accurate and strips 18% of the failures. The baseline it has to beat — releasing everything — is 90.5%. So the judge buys about one percentage point of precision in exchange for withholding one answer in ten, and 82% of what it withholds is correct.

The lift is also not statistically significant: the cluster-bootstrap interval on high-band accuracy (87.3–95.1%) contains the no-judge baseline outright. And most of the gap is not a prompt that needs another round. Of the 23 wrong answers it released on the calibrate split, a Sonnet diagnosis with the answer key in hand found 16 where the answer key itself is doubtful and 9 that are not detectable without it. Only 6 of 23 are both genuinely wrong and visibly wrong from the trace. That is the real headroom, and it is small.

01 · What shipped

End to end, on the branch guide-rail. Every piece is in the serving path, not a notebook.

The runtime

SERVING_RUNTIME=agent_sdk is now the default. One live ClaudeSDKClient is held per chat session (serving/sdk_session.py) and closed on delete, eviction and shutdown, so a conversation is one session across HTTP requests, exactly as the eval runner treats it.

The judge in the turn

serving/sdk_turn.py holds the pipeline's four stage frames until the judge has ruled, then emits them, a judge frame, and an answer frame carrying band and withheld. A low band sends an empty answer and redacted stage output on the wire and into the visible history — the retriever and calculator frames carry the answer too, which is how it leaked (§07).

The record survives it

A withheld answer is still measured: the trace row keeps the value, the band and p_correct, and the session keeps the real answer in its own context so a later turn that depends on a withheld one stays coherent.

Fails closed

A judge that cannot run withholds. A guard-rail that fails open is not one. The one exception, added this round, is a rate-limited call — that is no verdict, not a cautious one (§07).

The training loop

Five CLI subcommands — judge-dataset, judge-diagnose, judge-distil, judge-score, judge-gate — with the prompt lineage judge_prompts (j1, j2…), the alias judge_champion, an append-only gate ledger and the MLflow experiment convfinqa-judge.

Visible in the product

A band badge beside each released answer and a "not confident in this one" block in place of a withheld one; a judge panel on /admin/runtimes; a section on the published agent-sdk.html that story_check refuses to let go stale.

The 95% upper bound is one-sided Wilson on the unseen high-band error; the accuracy and capture CIs are cluster bootstraps resampling conversations, because a report's turns share a history and usually an error. Operating target for the band was ≤1% high-band error.

Read the trade the way a user would. On the test split the judge withholds 33 answers to remove 6 wrong ones. Twenty-seven of the 33 — 82% — were answers the agent had got right. The low band's precision is 18%, and the released set improves from 90.54% to 91.46%: +0.92pp, which the confidence interval does not separate from zero.

The two halves of the target are also in tension in a way worth naming. 99% high-band accuracy is a precision demand and is met by withholding aggressively; stripping 99% of failures is a recall demand and is met the same way — but every extra withholding also costs correct answers, and at this discrimination level (AUROC 0.52 on test) the judge cannot buy one without paying for the other roughly one-for-one. The risk–coverage curve says the same thing bluntly: no threshold on p_correct reaches 1% error at any coverage above zero (coverage_at_target = 0.0 on test, 0.66% on calibrate).

The probability is not a probability. p_correct is saturated: 263 of 349 test turns were scored exactly 0.99, and 300 of 349 sit at 0.97 or above. The judge is emitting a band with a decorative number attached, not a calibrated belief — which is why AUROC is 0.52 and ECE 0.11, and why thresholding buys nothing that the binary band does not already give.

04 · Why it misses

After scoring j1 on calibrate I ran round 2 of the loop: a Sonnet teacher re-diagnosed all 53 of the judge's own misses with the answer key and the judge's verdict in hand, and was asked, for each, whether a judge without the key could have known. That diagnosis is the honest account of the residual error.

"what was total operating income in 2013?" — predicted 318.0, key says 1.6. The question names no segment and the document discusses operating income in two separate places. A judge could flag the ambiguity; it could not know which the key picked.

Predicted 2.4 billion, key says 2.4. The trail cited the right sentence and mapped the right year. This is a formatting disagreement scored as a wrong answer.

And the false alarms are mostly the judge being right

Of the 30 correct answers j1 withheld on calibrate, the sampled reasons are almost all convention disputes where the trail genuinely does disagree with the key's presentation and the scorer forgives it: "question asks for 'percentage change' but the answer is reported as decimal (0.17) rather than percentage"; "cited table cells show −3.24 and −3.29 but the trail retrieves 3.24 and 3.29 — sign mismatch". The judge is reading the trace correctly and the scorer is being lenient. Both false alarms and released-wrongs, then, point at the same thing: on this dataset the label is not a clean enough target for a 1%-error guard-rail.

On lookups the judge is close to usable — 97.1% accurate on what it releases, catching 40% of failures at a cost of four withheld correct answers. On multi-step computations it is 88.6%, worse than releasing everything unjudged on that slice would be scored, and it catches one failure in seven. That is the shape of the problem: verifying "is this cell the one the question named" is tractable from a trace; verifying "is this the right two-step program for this question" is nearly the original task, and the judge is a smaller model than the one that got it wrong.

Round 2 changed nothing that mattered

The unpaired numbers make j2 look worse than j1 — 88.2% high-band accuracy against 91.4%. That is an artifact of which turns were judged before the session limit hit: those 129 turns are harder, and j1 itself scores 88.3% on them. Paired on the turns both judges actually scored, the comparison is flat:

The two judges disagree on 7 turns out of 129, and all seven are answers the agent had got right — four newly withheld, three newly released. Round 2 grew the prompt by 57% (14.9k → 23.4k characters) and moved zero wrong answers in either direction.

Neither version can be shown significant, and finishing the pass would not change that. The gate rule is deterministic — coverage must rise, capture must not fall, error must be inside 1% — and j2 has coverage flat-to-down with error at 11.8%, so it cannot qualify at any sample size. Scoring the remaining 175 turns would change the reason recorded in the ledger, not the verdict. And a real difference of a couple of points could not be resolved on a split this size in any case: j1's own +0.92pp over having no judge at all is not separable from zero on 349 turns, and the two judges disagree on 5% of turns and on none of the failures. The pass was stopped there by decision on 2026-09-08; j2's calibrate row is reported as the 129 turns it judged, marked incomplete, on the published page too.

07 · Two defects the round found, and four the review did

The first j2 calibrate pass hit the subscription session limit on turn 129 of 304. Every call after it raised a rate-limit refusal, the scorer failed each one closed to low — correct behaviour for a served turn, wrong for a measurement — and the pass reported coverage 36.2% with failure capture 56.7%. Read naively that is a judge that got dramatically more cautious and much better at catching failures. It is a spent session. The gate then compared those numbers against j1 on equal terms.

This is the rule s10 already established for the eval runner — a rate-limited turn is unscored, never wrong — which judge scoring did not inherit. Fixed in 01a3be7: score_split catches the rate limit separately from a genuine judge failure and writes unscored=True with no band; once the session is spent the remaining turns short-circuit rather than paying for the same refusal 175 more times; scored_only is the single door every consumer of band comes through, so an unscored turn is absent from the numerator and the denominator; and gate_judges now refuses such a file outright, naming it and the count, with no override. Serving is unchanged — a served answer with no verdict must still be withheld.

The verdict itself survived: paired properly (§06), j2 is indistinguishable from j1, so "rejected" was right. But a gate deciding on a number that describes the billing account rather than the model only stays harmless by luck.

The recording half of the fix then proved itself twice. Both re-scoring attempts hit the limit again — 235 of 304 unjudged, then 69 judged before the reset window closed — and both times the pass recorded unscored rows with no band, returned complete: false, and produced a file the gate refuses. The marker is also read out of error, so the original 2026-09-07 file — the one that caused this — is refused too, despite predating the column.

The short-circuit half did not work, and the review caught it. The check on the shared spent flag sat before the concurrency semaphore, and asyncio.gather runs every task's synchronous prefix before any task can complete — so all 304 rows had already passed the check by the time the first refusal came back, and every one of them still made its call. It looked like it was working only because a refusal returns fast: the 304-turn pass that judged 69 finished in seven minutes, which reads like a short-circuit and is really 235 quick rejections. Moved inside the semaphore so a task that waited for a permit re-checks after the session is known spent. Found by the no-mistakes review gate, not by me, and not by any test — the recorded output is identical either way, which is exactly why it went unnoticed.

What the review gate then found

The finished branch went through the no-mistakes pipeline on Sonnet 5. It raised five findings; all five were real, and two were holes in the feature's own contract rather than tidiness. They are listed here because a write-up that reported only the parts I found myself would be the wrong record.

FindingWhat was actually wrong
The withheld answer reached the browser contract The streaming endpoint forwarded every frame verbatim, and the retriever and calculator stage_output frames contain the final answer — emitted before the judge had ruled. So on a withheld turn the answer had already arrived. My abstention test used the non-streaming route and the streaming test checked only the answer frame, so nothing caught it. The withholding did not hold on the path the chat UI actually uses.
The coverage rule was inert contract "Coverage must not fall" was conditioned on the baseline meeting the error target. Neither judge meets 1% on this data, so the check never fired — j2 was rejected on the error-target check alone. The rule was weaker than it is documented to be.
"Never promoted on the test split" was a convention The refusal only fired if the caller volunteered evidence_split="test", and no call site did. The gate now derives the split from the scores CSV — a new trailing split column, with the filename as the fallback for the three already-committed files — refuses a test-split gate outright, and passes the derived value on to promotion.
The pipeline champion could be set to a judge prompt registry.promote refused an sdk version but not a judge one, so convfinqa-mlflow promote judge_j1 would have pointed champion at a prompt serving cannot build four agents from.
The rate-limit short-circuit never engaged Described above — the check sat outside the semaphore.

Three of the five are cases where an invariant this project states plainly in CLAUDE.md was enforced by discipline rather than by code. That is the pattern worth taking away: the judge's own guard-rails needed guard-rails.

The honest summary

The feature is complete, tested and serving: the runtime, the judge, the training loop, the registry lineage, the gate, the record, and the UI all exist and are covered by 356 passing backend tests and 140 frontend ones. What it does not do is hit 99/99. The high band's +0.92pp over having no judge is not statistically significant — the cluster-bootstrap interval on high-band accuracy, 87.3–95.1%, contains the 90.5% no-judge baseline — and the one round of optimisation moved seven bands, none of them a wrong answer. The measurement says the ceiling here is set by the answer key, not the prompt: 6 of 23 released-wrong answers are cases any judge without the key could have caught. So the conclusion is the one you drew — as a gate it is not worth it. I would ship the band as advice, split the policy by turn type, and fix the labels before spending another round on the prompt.
