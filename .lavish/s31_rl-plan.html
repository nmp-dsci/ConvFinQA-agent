01

Is this a good project for RL?

RL works when three things hold: a cheap, trustworthy reward; a model that sometimes succeeds; and failures that come from the model's habits rather than from missing knowledge. All three hold here, with the caveats below.

why it fits
  • A free, exact reward. Every question has a gold answer, and numeric_match already scores it. No reward model, no judge.
  • Measured headroom. On clean program turns, 82% right on one try and 90% on any of four (§04).
  • SFT has plateaued. Val went flat after about one epoch, and sft2 against epoch 2 is +1.1 on test, not significant.
  • The errors are the student's own. Most are wrong operand values (18 first mistakes on test, against Flash's 7) or operands in the wrong order. Flash rarely makes these mistakes, so SFT on Flash's turns never shows the student its own. RL does.
  • A small, cheap model. A 4B student can sample about 280 tries per step on one GPU.
where it can't help
  • Questions it never gets right. 10% of clean program turns on val were wrong on all four tries. With no right try there is no signal. Those need better data or a stronger teacher.
  • Bad gold. An always-wrong question may be a label error. It produces no gradient, so it does little harm, but it caps the ceiling.
  • Knock-on errors. 24 of the 59 wrong program turns on test are knock-ons from an earlier wrong answer. RL trains on clean history, so it fixes these only by fixing the first mistake.
what to expect
  • RL mostly turns sometimes-right into usually-right. Single-try accuracy (pass@1) rises toward best-of-k (pass@k), while best-of-k at large k doesn't grow (Yue et al., 2025).
  • Clean program turns: 82% toward about 88%. Overall val: +2 to +5 points. Test 100: +1 to +4.
  • It probably won't pass Flash (87.7% on test). It should close part of the 6.3-point gap.
  • The honest risk: a real +3 has about even odds of reading as not significant on test 100, whose 95% CI is about ±3.5 points.
02

How RL works, from zero

SFT says "do what the teacher did". RL says "do more of what worked for you, and less of what didn't". The model is graded on its own attempts, not on someone else's.

The policy and the episodeThe policy is the student. An episode is one question: the student calls the calculator tools one or more times, then sends StructuredOutput. That is about 450 tokens over 2 model calls.
Sample a groupFor one question, with the same conversation so far, the student answers 8 times at temperature 0.7. The tries differ because sampling is random.
GradeEach try gets reward 1 if its answer matches gold, else 0. No human and no judge are involved.
Compare within the groupEach try's advantage is (reward − group mean) ÷ group standard deviation. Tries better than average get a positive weight; worse ones get a negative weight.
Update, then repeatRaise the probability of every token in a positive try and lower it in a negative one, in proportion to the advantage. Then sample again with the updated student. That is GRPO, from DeepSeekMath (2024).

A worked example from val

Single_ETR/2008/page_153.pdf-2, q2: "what was, then, the change in amount over the year, in millions?" Gold is −17.4, which is subtract(19.2, 36.6). Two of the four sft2 checkpoints answered −17.4. The other two answered 17.4, with the operands reversed.

Say 8 tries give 5 right and 3 wrong (an illustration). The mean is 0.625 and the standard deviation 0.48, so:

right: (1 − 0.625) ÷ 0.48 = +0.77
wrong: (0 − 0.625) ÷ 0.48 = −1.29

Tokens all 8 tries share, such as reading the table and finding 19.2 and 36.6, get 5 × 0.77 − 3 × 1.29 ≈ 0 net push: they cancel. The gradient lands only where the tries differ, which here is the order of the operands. That is how RL assigns credit without being told which token was wrong.

If all 8 tries are right, or all 8 wrong, every advantage is 0 and the question teaches nothing. That's why the share of questions that flip matters more than the dataset's size.

One formula, three methods

All three take a step along Σ weight × ∇ log π(token). They differ in who wrote the tokens and how each is weighted.

MethodTokens written byWeight on a try
SFT sft1, sft2Flash, the teacher1 on every token
Rejection sampling (RFT) s30's sft3 ideathe student1 if right, 0 if wrong
GRPO this planthe studentthe advantage: + if better than its group, − if worse

RFT is RL without negative weights. GRPO adds them, so a wrong try actively pushes its tokens down instead of being ignored.

Your temperature question, answered by RL. In SFT the sampling temperature never enters training. In RL it is the exploration knob. At temperature 0 all 8 tries are identical, every advantage is 0, and nothing is learned. So we train at the serving temperature, 0.7, and RL improves the exact policy we serve.

rule of thumb
What matters is how many questions the student sometimes gets right. RL can move a model with very few such questions (one training example improved math reasoning in Wang et al., 2025). But each try carries only about 1 bit, so you need many tries per question, not many questions.
05

The design

An episode is one question, continued on the student's own history. The reward is the eval scorer plus a sign check. The update is plain on-policy GRPO, with no extra tricks until we need them.

An episode: branch and continue

How it works. For each conversation, try q0 8 times, keep one right try at random as the history, try q1 8 times on top of it, and so on. Stop at a question with no right try. It's the repo's own stop-at-first-wrong rule, applied per try.

Why. GRPO's tries must share their context, so an episode is one question. The history is the student's own, as at serving, so there is no teacher-written context to drift from. And it trains where errors start, on first mistakes, not knock-ons.

Not adopted. One reward for the whole conversation: one number for 3 to 6 answers muddies the credit, and after q0 the tries no longer share a context. Flash's recorded history: it exists only for the 1,152 collected conversations, and it is in the teacher's voice, not the student's.

The code already does this. open_agent._ask(agent, prompt, history) answers one question on a given history with the checked harness. Calling it 8 times on one history is a group.

The reward

A try gets 1 if the eval scorer accepts its answer and the answer's sign agrees with gold, and 0 otherwise. A question with no valid reply after the harness's attempts gets 0.

The scorer has a blind spot RL would find. numeric_match rule 1 accepts any two numbers that round to the same integer, so 0.5 matches −0.5, and 0.18 matches −0.18. Across 14 recorded passes, 19 of 6,984 answers scored right had the opposite sign to gold, including one in each of sft2-e1's val and test passes (gold 0.5 against −0.5, and 0.1797 against −0.1797). In an eval that's noise. But RL optimises the reward directly, so it would never learn to fix a sign error on a small value. The reward therefore adds a sign check. The eval metric stays as it is, so every recorded number stays comparable.

Not adopted: partial credit for right operand values. It targets the biggest failure, but partial rewards are where a policy learns to game its grader. Start binary.

06

What it runs on, and how that differs from SFT

Mostly the SFT setup: the same base model and LoRA, the same Modal volumes, vLLM 0.21.0, and the same serving app for scoring. Three things change: generation runs inside the training loop, the GPU is bigger, and the run is longer.

The loop. The driver runs on your Mac, like every eval pass, because the real pydantic_ai_checked harness, the scorer, the data and MLflow are all here. It sends chat completions to a vLLM server inside the H100 container that serves the current adapter, and sends each graded batch to the trainer in the same container. The trainer takes one step, saves the adapter and loads it into vLLM (VLLM_ALLOW_RUNTIME_LORA_UPDATING, /v1/load_lora_adapter). Every 25 steps the adapter goes to the runs volume, where the existing Student app scores it on val.

Why the eval harness is the environment. The student is scored through Pydantic AI, vLLM's hermes tool parser and Qwen's chat template. If RL generated through a different loop, the student would be trained in one format and served in another. TRL's own tool loop, for example, swaps in a patched training template. That's the kind of silent mismatch s28's data bug was. Here the rollouts are the eval harness. The trainer re-renders each try with the same template and the SFT mask code, and a parity check proves the tokens match before step 1.

Where the GPU time goes

Each try sits in about 7k tokens of context: the system prompt and tool schemas (4,587 tokens, counted with the student's tokenizer), the report and first question (+1,116), about 630 tokens per earlier question, and its own reply of about 450 tokens.

Generation is cheap here, because the shared context is prefix-cached and replies are short. The bill is the trainer's forward and backward pass over that context. Dropping all-right and all-wrong groups before training cuts it by about two-thirds.

Why an H100 when SFT used an L4?

Per trained token they cost about the same: about $0.24 per million on the H100 against $0.29 on the L4, assuming the H100 is 6× faster. The L4 measured 760 tokens/s on sft2. The H100 has room for vLLM and the trainer together, and it finishes in hours instead of days.

The first measured step replaces the 6× assumption, as sft2's step-1 run did. Modal prices here come from third-party listings; check modal.com/pricing before the run.

Options for the training stack

recommended

B · The eval harness as the environment, with our own GRPO step

Train equals serve by construction. It reuses open_agent, sft/rows, the SFT mask code and the scorer. The GRPO step is about 150 lines you can read end to end.

Cost: we own the trainer's correctness. The parity check and a one-step sanity test cover it.

A · TRL's GRPOTrainer, all inside Modal

TRL now runs multi-turn tool loops (tools=, and an experimental environment_factory), with maintained loss variants and correction for mismatch between vLLM and the trainer. Modal has a GRPO-with-TRL example.

Cost: its loop ends a rollout on a reply with no tool call, but our turns end on a StructuredOutput call. It swaps in its own Qwen3 training template, and H1–H3 and the re-ask would have to be rebuilt. A good fallback if B's trainer fights us.

C · A managed RL service

Thinking Machines' Tinker and similar services run the GPUs, and you write the loop.

Cost: it isn't Modal, and I haven't checked that it supports this model. Listed for completeness only.

08

How we'll know it worked

What replaces the loss curve

Logged at every step:

  • train reward, and the share of groups that flip
  • accuracy by turn type (program, number)
  • token entropy, and KL to sft2-e1
  • reply tokens and calculator calls per question
  • H1–H3 retry rate, and the no-valid-reply rate
  • the log-prob gap between vLLM and the trainer
  • seconds and dollars per step

Reward up with entropy steady is learning. Reward up with entropy collapsing is memorising one way to answer. Reward up with val flat is a train/serve gap or hacking.

Choosing and comparing

  • Selection: each checkpoint gets 2 val passes, and the pick is the best mean.
  • Comparison: the pick gets 4 passes against sft2-e1's 4 from P0, compared question by question on the mean of the passes, with a cluster bootstrap by conversation. Averaging cuts the sampling noise that made two passes of the same weights disagree on 31 to 33 program questions.
  • Test 100: once, for the val pick, on your word. Its 95% CI is about ±3.5 points, so a true +3 has roughly even odds of reading as significant.
  • Hacking audit: at every checkpoint, read 20 questions that went from wrong to right, and check that the reasoning, program and calculator calls support the answer.
